ChatGPT Can Now Spend Your Money. Here Is What Face ID Does Not Check.

On November 13, OpenAI gave its AI agents the power to pay. ChatGPT Wallet lets them complete purchases for you, with tokenized cards and a biometric check before every payment. The design is solid, but Face ID only proves who approved, not whether the agent chose well. Here is how it works, where the real risk sits and how to use it safely.

From Recommending to Paying

For two years, AI assistants have been able to find a product, compare prices and fill a cart. The last step, paying, stayed with you. On November 13, 2026, OpenAI changed that.

ChatGPT Wallet lets OpenAI's "dots" agents complete purchases on your behalf. Before each payment, you approve it with Face ID or Touch ID. The agent never sees your real card number.

It is a small feature on paper. In practice, it is the moment AI agents stop being advisers and start being economic actors, and it moves agent security from "embarrassing" to "expensive."

How ChatGPT Wallet Works

According to OpenAI's help center, the flow is simple: the agent finds what you asked for, prepares the order, and shows you a summary. You review it and confirm with a biometric check on a supported device. Only then does the agent pay.

What OpenAI Got Right

01
Tokenization

The Agent Cannot Leak What It Never Had

Because the agent works with a token instead of the card number, a compromised or manipulated agent cannot paste your card details into a fake checkout or a chat log. That removes the most obvious way to steal a card through an AI agent.

02
Human in the loop

A Biometric Gate on Every Purchase

For now, no money moves without a human tap. That is a real control, and much better than what many agent tools do today: asking users to paste card details into a conversation, or saving them in a browser profile the agent fully controls.

Other AI companies will likely copy this model. Tokenized credentials plus strong customer authentication is how mobile wallets made phone payments safe, and it is a sensible starting point for agents.

Where It Gets Hard

The biometric check proves that you approved. It does not prove that what you approved is what you wanted.

An agent that shops browses the open web: product pages, reviews, comparison sites, sponsored listings. Any of them can contain text written for the agent, not for you. This is indirect prompt injection, and it is the main open problem in agent security.

  • A poisoned product page tells the agent that another seller is "the official store."
  • Hidden text pushes a bigger quantity, a premium version or an add-on.
  • A fake review steers the agent toward a scam merchant with a convincing checkout.

In each case, you see a clean, plausible summary and confirm it in two seconds. The biometric check works perfectly, and you still pay for the wrong thing.

The real risk is the next step. OpenAI says it expects users to eventually delegate purchases within spending limits. When that happens, the per-purchase human check becomes optional, and the agent's judgment becomes the main control.

Three Questions Nobody Has Fully Answered

QuestionWhy it matters
Who is liable?If an agent buys the wrong item after reading a manipulated page, is it the user who approved, OpenAI whose agent chose, or the merchant who hosted the content?
How do chargebacks work?Card disputes assume a human made a mistake or was defrauded. "My agent was tricked" is a new category that issuers will have to handle.
How do merchants tell agents apart?Stores fight bots every day. They now need to welcome legitimate agents while still blocking fraud bots that look the same.

How to Use It Safely

HabitWhy
1. Read the full summaryCheck seller, quantity, options and shipping, not only the total, before you tap.
2. Use a low-limit cardA card with a low limit, or a virtual card number, caps the damage of a bad purchase.
3. Turn on notificationsGet an alert from your bank for every transaction, so a surprise shows up in seconds.
4. Prefer known merchantsAsk the agent to buy only from stores you name. It shrinks the space a malicious page can exploit.
5. Wait on spending limitsWhen pre-approved purchases arrive, start with small limits and narrow categories.

The Fine Print

Early days. ChatGPT Wallet is rolling out gradually and only in the United States. Details on fraud protection, disputes and liability may change. The prompt injection scenarios in this article describe known risks for shopping agents in general, not documented incidents involving ChatGPT Wallet.

On November 13, 2026, OpenAI launched ChatGPT Wallet, which lets its agents complete purchases for US ChatGPT Pro users with tokenized cards and a Face ID or Touch ID check on every payment. The design is solid, but the biometric check confirms who approved, not whether the agent chose well, and that gap will grow when spending limits replace per-purchase approval.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is ChatGPT Wallet?
A payment feature from OpenAI that lets its AI agents complete purchases on your behalf, after you approve each one with Face ID or Touch ID.
Who can use it?
ChatGPT Pro subscribers in the United States, 18 or older, with an eligible US-issued American Express, Chase, Mastercard or Visa card. The rollout is gradual.
Can the agent see my card number?
No. According to OpenAI, it keeps tokenized card credentials and does not store full card details, so the agent never has the real number.
Is it safe?
The payment side is well protected. The bigger risk is the agent being steered by manipulated web content into buying the wrong thing, which you could then approve without noticing.
Can the agent buy without asking me?
Not today. OpenAI says pre-approved purchases within spending limits are planned but not available yet.

My Take

Payment is where agent security stops being theoretical. A prompt injection that leaks a summary is embarrassing. One that triggers a payment is fraud. OpenAI built the payment rail well: tokens, biometrics, a human in the loop. The weak point is the part no biometric can check: the agent's judgment about what to buy, and from whom.

As a network security engineer, I see the same lesson every time a new system gets the power to act: controls have to follow the money. Read the summary, cap the card, watch the notifications, and do not hand over spending limits until the industry has answered who pays when an agent is fooled.
