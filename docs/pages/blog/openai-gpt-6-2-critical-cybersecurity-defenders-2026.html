GPT-6.2 Crossed the Critical Cybersecurity Line. Here Is What Defenders Should Do.

OpenAI's own system card says GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol meet the Critical threshold for cybersecurity capabilities. Working exploit success jumped from 31.5% to 53.5% in one release, and even the cheaper model is close behind. Here is what the numbers mean, where the guardrails stop, and how defenders should respond.

The Vendor Rang the Alarm Itself

On November 17, 2026, OpenAI released two new models: GPT-6.2 Astra, built for demanding work in science, law and finance, and GPT-6.2 Sol, built for fast everyday tasks. Both are available in ChatGPT, Codex and the API.

The launch post talks about benchmarks and lower prices. The system card says something more important for security teams:

"Both models meet the Critical threshold for Cybersecurity capabilities and the High threshold for Biological and Chemical capabilities under our Preparedness Framework."

In plain words: the company that built these models says they can meaningfully help with serious offensive cyber operations. This is not a critic's warning. It is the vendor's own rating.

It is also not the first time. OpenAI already rated GPT-6 Astra Critical in September. What changed with GPT-6.2 is how much further the capability moved in a single release.

53.5% GPT-6.2 Astra mean arbitrary code execution rate on OpenAI's internal exploit benchmark
50.5% GPT-6.2 Sol on the same test, the cheaper and faster model
31.5% GPT-6 Astra, the previous generation, on the same test
88.6% Astra success rate in UK AISI vulnerability discovery testing (GPT-6 Astra: 81.7%)

What the Numbers Actually Say

OpenAI tested both models without production safeguards, using several elicitation methods, and says the results "represent a lower bound on potential capabilities." The key measurements on the cyber side:

Two details stand out. First, the jump in working code execution, from 31.5% to 53.5%, is the number that matters most to defenders, because it measures exploits that actually run, not just bugs that get found. The system card puts it simply: both models "were better than GPT-6 Astra and GPT-6.1 Sol at developing working exploits from known software vulnerabilities."

Second, Sol is almost as capable as Astra on exploitation, at a fraction of the price: $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens, against $8 and $40 for Astra, according to The Decoder. Strong offensive capability is no longer reserved for the most expensive tier.

Not every result went up. On Irregular's FrontierCyber challenges, Astra solved 68 of 217, fewer than GPT-6 Astra's 77. Progress is real, but uneven.

The Guardrails, and Where They Stop

OpenAI did not ship these models without changes. The system card describes several layers:

  • Tighter defaults: "we tightened default restrictions on higher-risk cybersecurity assistance and require additional vetting for more permissive access."
  • Misuse monitoring: because both models meet the Critical threshold, a monitoring system watches external use, including "scaled agentic vulnerability research and chained exploit development for users outside of our trusted access program."
  • Trusted access (Daybreak): Daybreak Blue reduces refusals on general-purpose models. Daybreak Red requires separate approval and stronger verification. For GPT-6.2, only Daybreak Red organizations get reduced cyber refusals on Astra.
  • Red-teaming: an automated campaign of about 1.1 million GPU-hours found two high-severity jailbreaks, which OpenAI says it mitigated.

These are serious measures. They also have limits that the system card itself documents:

From the system card. The UK AI Security Institute "identified a substantial regression in robustness relative to GPT-6 Astra" and built a universal jailbreak in half as many queries as for the previous model. OpenAI says the issue is mitigated, but UK AISI had not validated that fix at the time of writing.

And guardrails only protect one front door. Attackers do not need this exact model. Open-weight models, jailbreaks and self-hosted agent frameworks keep closing the gap. This week, Google already caught AI agents turning security patches into live exploits with no human in the loop, and the FBI warned about the ARTEX framework reaching privileged access in under three hours.

Five Moves for Defenders

01
Threat model

Assume the Capability Is Already Out There

Plan as if Critical-level offensive help is available to your adversaries, with or without OpenAI's safeguards. Update your threat model so "automated, adaptive attacker" is the baseline scenario, not the exception.

02
Patching

Move From Weeks to Days

When a model can turn a published fix into working code execution, "we patch within 30 days" becomes a liability. Critical, internet-facing assets need a days-not-weeks patch SLA, with extra monitoring until the fix is in place.

03
Speed

Measure Your Response Time in Hours

If an AI-driven intrusion can reach privileged access in under three hours, a detection and response cycle measured in days is a fight between a machine and a calendar. Know your real mean time to detect and contain, and shrink it.

04
Automation

Use AI on the Defense Side Too

Detection engineering, alert triage, log correlation and asset inventory are where defensive AI pays off fastest. The goal is not to replace analysts. It is to cut the noise so they spend their time on real decisions.

05
Fundamentals

Close the Same Old Doors

Most AI-assisted attacks still land on exposed services, missing MFA, flat networks and over-privileged accounts. Network segmentation, least privilege and solid logging are not old-school. They are what slows an automated attacker down long enough for you to see it.

The Tension Nobody Should Ignore

The same restrictions that block attackers also slow down defenders. OpenAI itself acknowledges that the tighter safeguards will limit some legitimate vulnerability research and security testing. Pentesters, red teams and vulnerability researchers will hit more refusals unless their organization goes through trusted access vetting.

The practical advice: if your team relies on AI for security research, talk to your AI vendors now about verified access programs. Do not discover the refusal policy in the middle of an incident.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does "Critical" mean for cybersecurity?
It is the highest level in OpenAI's Preparedness Framework for cyber capabilities. OpenAI uses it for models that can meaningfully help with serious offensive cyber operations, which triggers stricter safeguards and monitoring.
What is the difference between GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol?
Astra targets demanding specialized work and is the more capable and expensive model. Sol is designed for fast everyday tasks and is cheaper. Both reached the Critical cybersecurity threshold.
Were the tests run with safeguards?
No. OpenAI says it tested the models without production safeguards and that the results represent a lower bound on potential capabilities.
What is Daybreak?
OpenAI's trusted access program for cybersecurity. Daybreak Blue reduces refusals on general-purpose models. Daybreak Red requires stronger verification and is the only tier with reduced cyber refusals on GPT-6.2 Astra.
What should a security team do first?
Review patch timelines for internet-facing systems, measure real detection and response times, and check that MFA, segmentation and least privilege are actually enforced.

My Take

"Critical" is not a headline to panic about. It is a signal to re-check assumptions. The threat model changed, and the vendor said it out loud. Patching SLAs, network segmentation and response speed should change with it.
