Nvidia Is in Talks to Buy a Piece of Perplexity. In March, It Said It Was Done Investing.

A reported round would value Perplexity above $30 billion. The revenue underneath it is real and growing fast. What the valuation actually measures is the harder question, because the investor discussing it is also the supplier being paid.

13 min read
August 31, 2026
By Kodjo Apedoh

What Was Actually Reported

On August 23, 2026, The Information reported that Nvidia is in discussions to take an equity stake in Perplexity as part of a funding round valuing the AI search company at more than $30 billion. Reuters, PYMNTS and Benzinga picked it up over the following days. Neither company has confirmed it.

Treat the $30 billion as reported rather than settled. Rounds at this size are negotiated for weeks and repriced more than once before anyone signs. What is not in dispute is the operating performance underneath the talks, and that is the part worth spending time on.

$30B+ Reported valuation in the round under discussion
$750M Perplexity annualised revenue, up from under $250M in January
$40B+ Nvidia equity bets in AI companies during 2026
$6B Paid to license AI development software from Poolside, weeks earlier

The Business Underneath the Number

Perplexity's annualised revenue has climbed above $750 million, up from under $250 million at the start of 2026. That is a tripling in roughly eight months, and it is the single most defensible fact in the entire story, because it is money customers chose to pay.

What is more interesting is where it came from. Not search.

The reported driver is Perplexity Computer, a cloud-hosted agent that operates a machine on the user's behalf: running research across many sources, filling in forms, filing documents, executing multi-step tasks that used to require a person sitting in front of a browser. The company sells it to professional users, not consumers browsing for free.

Why the product shift matters more than the growth rate. Answer engines monetise like media: attention, subscriptions, thin margins, permanent competition with a free alternative that has better distribution. Agents monetise like labour: they are priced against the cost of the hour they replace, buyers are departments rather than individuals, and usage rises with the value of the task instead of the volume of clicks. Perplexity did not grow three times faster at search. It changed what it was selling.

The Nvidia relationship predates all of this. Nvidia has held a position in Perplexity since 2023. In July 2026, Perplexity committed to running its agent workloads on Nvidia's Vera CPUs. Jensen Huang has said publicly that Perplexity is his preferred chatbot. Reporting also indicates Nvidia at one point weighed a multibillion-dollar licensing arrangement with the company rather than an equity stake.

This is not a first date. It is a supplier deciding whether to become a shareholder in a customer it has already backed, already sold to, and already publicly endorsed.

The Part That Does Not Fit

On March 4, 2026, at the Morgan Stanley Technology, Media and Telecom conference, Jensen Huang said Nvidia's investments in OpenAI and Anthropic would likely be its last.

What has happened since:

  • Poolside. Nvidia agreed to pay roughly $6 billion to license AI development software, while separately investing $1 billion at a $12 billion pre-money valuation.
  • Perplexity. Equity talks at a valuation above $30 billion, reported in late August.
  • The running total. Nvidia's equity bets crossed $40 billion during 2026, led by a roughly $30 billion position in OpenAI.

Five months is a short retirement. The charitable reading is that Huang was describing the end of a specific category of mega-investment in frontier labs, not a blanket stop. The less charitable reading is that a company whose revenue depends on its customers' ability to keep buying cannot easily stop helping them buy.

01
The Structural Read

Nvidia Has Become the Central Bank of the AI Economy

A central bank supplies the liquidity that lets an economy transact, and it holds positions across the institutions that depend on it. That is now a fair description of Nvidia's role. It provides capital to AI startups, those startups buy or lease Nvidia silicon, and the capital returns to Nvidia as revenue. The chips are the currency, the equity stakes are the reserves, and the customer base is the money supply. This is not a metaphor anyone at Nvidia would endorse, but it explains behaviour that is otherwise hard to read: why a company with a dominant market position and no obvious need for venture exposure keeps writing venture cheques, and why it kept writing them after saying it would stop.

Circular Financing, Stated Plainly

The pattern has a name in the market now, and it is not a flattering one.

MIT Sloan professor Michael Cusumano described the central paradox in the simplest available terms: Nvidia invests billions in OpenAI stock, and OpenAI announces it will buy billions of dollars of Nvidia chips. The same shape recurs across the buildout, with Microsoft, Oracle, CoreWeave and now a set of application-layer companies wired into the same loop.

Two credible camps read it differently, and both are arguing about the same transaction.

The Bear Case

Wedbush analyst Matthew Bryson placed Nvidia's investments and buildouts "squarely into the circular investment theme" driving concerns about the durability of the rally. The mechanical worry is straightforward: revenue booked against capital you supplied is not the same quality of revenue as cash from an independently funded buyer. If one participant stumbles, the exposure runs in both directions at once. Buy from me, I invest in you, and everything is fine unless either of us has a problem, in which case both of us do.

The Bull Case

Asset manager Janus Henderson has described the wave of AI deals as closer to a virtuous circle, lining up suppliers, builders and customers to meet genuine demand for compute. There is a serious argument here. Capital-intensive industries have always paired long-term purchase commitments with vendor financing, because that is how you get a factory built before the customer exists. Aircraft, telecoms, semiconductors and energy have all done it. Vendor financing is a normal instrument, not a red flag by itself.

This is not fraud, and it is not new. Nothing described here is hidden, illegal, or unusual for an industry building physical capacity faster than customer cash flow can fund it. The concern is narrower and more specific: circular structures make demand look more independent than it is, and they concentrate correlated risk in a small number of balance sheets. Both of those are problems of measurement and concentration, not of intent.
02
The Signal Problem

Valuation Has Quietly Stopped Being an Independent Reading

The reason a funding round carries information is that an outside party, with its own money and no stake in the outcome, looked at a business and priced it. Strip out the independence and the number stops being a measurement and becomes a position. When the investor setting the price is also the supplier being paid out of the proceeds, the valuation is partly a statement about that supplier's own order book. It may still be correct. It is simply no longer evidence in the way the market habitually reads it, and the habit has not adjusted.

What Would Actually Break It

The circle holds as long as end demand grows into the capacity being financed. Three things would test it.

A quarter that misses. Not Nvidia's. One of the funded companies. The moment a well-capitalised AI company reports materially below plan, every position built on the assumption of compounding demand gets repriced at once, and the correlation everyone described as a virtuous circle becomes the reason the drawdown is synchronised.

The debt market, not the equity market. Equity investors can wait. Lenders cannot. Reporting through 2026 has already noted circular deal structures souring sentiment in AI-adjacent credit. Financing costs are the earliest honest signal here, because a lender has no upside from optimism.

Agent revenue proving thinner than it looks. Perplexity's growth is real, but agent products are young and the churn behaviour of a professional agent subscription over two or three years is genuinely unknown. If retention disappoints across the category, the revenue multiple supporting these valuations moves before the revenue does.

What This Means If You Build on These Models

1. Vendor Solvency Is Now a Supply-Chain Question

If your product depends on an AI vendor's API, that vendor's funding structure is part of your risk surface, in the same way a single-source hardware supplier is. The question is not whether they are well funded today. It is who funded them, whether that funder is also their largest cost, and what happens to your service if that relationship changes. This belongs in vendor review alongside uptime and data handling, and almost nowhere is it there yet.

2. Read Valuations With the Cap Table Attached

A $30 billion mark set by a strategic investor who sells the company its infrastructure tells you something different from the same mark set by an unrelated fund. Neither is automatically wrong. But when you are benchmarking your own pricing, fundraising or partnership terms against public valuation news, check who set the price before treating it as a market signal.

3. The Revenue Moved to Agents, and That Is the Actual Lesson

The most transferable fact in this story has nothing to do with Nvidia. A company tripled its revenue in eight months by moving from answering questions to performing tasks. That is the clearest available evidence of where willingness to pay currently sits, and it is available to anyone building in this space regardless of what happens to the funding round.

4. Cheap Inference May Be Financed Rather Than Earned

Some of the pricing you enjoy today reflects genuine efficiency gains, and some of it reflects capital subsidising customer acquisition. Those two look identical on an invoice and behave completely differently under stress. If your unit economics only work at current API prices, model what happens at prices set by a market that has stopped subsidising, because that is the scenario worth having an answer to.

The number that will actually settle this. Not the round, and not Perplexity's next revenue milestone. It is the first quarter in which a major AI company misses its plan, and the market gets to observe how much of the circle was structure and how much was sentiment. Until that happens, every participant is being marked against assumptions that no one in the loop has an incentive to question.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Nvidia definitely investing in Perplexity?
No. As of late August 2026, The Information reported that talks were under way for Nvidia to take an equity stake in a round valuing Perplexity at more than $30 billion, and the report was picked up widely. Neither company has confirmed the deal, and terms at this stage are frequently renegotiated. Treat both the participation and the valuation as reported rather than final.
How much revenue does Perplexity actually make?
Perplexity's annualised revenue is reported above $750 million, up from under $250 million at the beginning of 2026. Annualised revenue extrapolates a recent period across a full year, so it runs ahead of trailing revenue actually collected. The reported driver of the increase is Perplexity Computer, a cloud-based agent that automates computer tasks for professional users, rather than the consumer search product.
What is circular AI financing?
It describes arrangements in which a supplier invests capital in a customer who then spends that capital buying the supplier's products, so the money returns as revenue. In AI, the most cited example is Nvidia investing in companies that commit to large purchases of Nvidia hardware. Supporters call it vendor financing that de-risks capacity buildout; critics argue it makes demand appear more independent than it is and concentrates correlated risk across a small number of balance sheets.
Did Jensen Huang say Nvidia would stop investing?
At the Morgan Stanley technology conference on March 4, 2026, Huang indicated that Nvidia's investments in OpenAI and Anthropic would likely be its last. Since then Nvidia agreed to pay roughly $6 billion to license software from Poolside alongside a $1 billion investment at a $12 billion pre-money valuation, and is now reported to be in equity talks with Perplexity. Nvidia's total equity positions in AI companies crossed $40 billion during 2026.
Is this an AI bubble?
The revenue growth at companies like Perplexity is real and paid by customers, which is not what a pure bubble looks like. The genuine concern is narrower: when suppliers finance their own customers, valuations stop functioning as independent evidence about demand, and the risk across those positions becomes correlated. That is a measurement and concentration problem. It becomes a solvency problem only if end demand fails to grow into the capacity being financed.
What should a business using AI vendors take from this?
Add funding structure to vendor risk review. Identify who financed your critical AI vendors, whether that financier is also the vendor's largest cost, and what your fallback is if that relationship changes or pricing resets. Also stress-test your unit economics against inference prices set without capital subsidy, since some current pricing reflects customer acquisition rather than achieved efficiency.

My Take

The Perplexity number I trust is $750 million, because customers paid it. Tripling annualised revenue in eight months by shipping an agent product is a genuine commercial result, and it is the most useful signal in this story for anyone building software right now.

The number I do not know how to read is $30 billion, because the investor discussing it is also the supplier being paid. That is not an accusation. Vendor financing is ordinary, disclosed and often rational. But it means the valuation has stopped being an independent reading of the AI market and has become, in part, a statement Nvidia is making about its own order book.

What bothers me is not the deal. It is the March statement. A company said it was finished with this category of investment and then did roughly $7 billion more of it within five months. The most likely explanation is not dishonesty. It is that Nvidia's position makes stopping genuinely difficult, because its revenue depends on customers whose ability to buy depends on capital that Nvidia is uniquely positioned to supply. That is a structural constraint, not a choice, and structural constraints are exactly what people underestimate when they assume a market can slow down gracefully.

The circle is not irrational. It is what a market looks like when one supplier is so far ahead that even the companies competing with it have to route capital, capacity and credibility through it on the way. It works beautifully in one direction and unwinds just as efficiently in the other.

When your largest supplier becomes your largest investor, who is left to validate the price?
